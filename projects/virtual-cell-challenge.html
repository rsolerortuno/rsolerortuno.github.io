Fourteen tries at a virtual cell

My first time with perturbation data: the Virtual Cell Challenge 2026, version by version

A plain-English retrospective of the Pedrito Amor entry to the Virtual Cell Challenge 2026: what each version tried, what worked, what broke and what I learned.

Competition retrospective · October 2026 Team name: Pedrito Amor

Before anything else: this was my first time working with perturbation data, and my first time thinking seriously about the idea of a “virtual cell”. I’m not an expert in this area. I signed up for the Virtual Cell Challenge mostly because I wanted an excuse to get my hands dirty with these datasets and understand how they actually behave, beyond what the papers say.

So this isn’t a victory lap. It’s a diary of fourteen versions, written so that anyone can follow it, including the parts where I got things wrong. Quite a lot of it is about getting things wrong.

The challenge, in plain words

Every cell in your body carries the same set of genes. You can think of a gene as a recipe card. What makes a liver cell different from a T cell is which recipes it’s actually cooking, and how much of each. Scientists measure this as gene expression: for every cell, a count of how many copies of each recipe are in use right now.

Now, there’s a lab technique called CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) that lets you quietly switch off one gene. When you switch one off, it rarely stays a local event. Other genes react: some go up, some go down, many don’t care. That whole pattern of reactions is called a perturbation response.

The challenge asked a simple-sounding question. If I show you a new type of cell, but only in its normal, untreated state, can you predict what will happen to all its genes when each of a list of target genes is switched off?

The catch is that you never see the new cell type being perturbed. You only see its untreated “control” cells, plus whatever experiments other people have run on other cell types. And the answer can’t be a vague description: you have to hand in a big table of whole-number counts for hundreds of simulated cells per target gene. Basically, fake but realistic single-cell data.

How the score works

The leaderboard combined six measurements into a single number. I’ll be honest, it took me a while to really understand what each of them rewards, and much of this story is about them pulling in different directions.

PDS

Can you tell the targets apart? If switching off gene A and gene B produce different responses, your predictions should be different too, and recognisably so.

MSE

How close are your predicted expression levels to the real ones? This is the "did you get the numbers right" metric.

NMAE

A second closeness check, based on the average size of each miss rather than the squared miss.

Fidelity

For the genes that really change, did you get the direction right? Up when it goes up, down when it goes down.

Reach

Of all the genes that really changed, how many did your prediction actually catch?

Jaccard

How much does your list of "changed genes" overlap with the real list? Mine hovered around zero the whole time.

Each measurement is normalised against a simple reference prediction, so 0 means “no better than the reference” and higher is better. That means the overall 0.2166 I got with v12 is not 21.66% accuracy. It’s a blend of six relative improvements. And if your raw error is worse than the reference, your MSE score is simply clipped to zero. For my first five versions, it was exactly that: zero.

How one prediction gets built

Almost every version used the same assembly line. What changed from version to version was one or two stations on it. I’ll refer back to this picture a lot.

  1. 1

    Look at the new cell at rest

    Its untreated control cells give the starting expression levels and how much they naturally vary.

    always
  2. 2

    Borrow measured responses

    Find experiments where the same gene was switched off in other cell types.

    v1, v2, v6, v8–v10
  3. 3

    Combine and weigh them

    Decide how much to trust each source, gene by gene.

    v6, v8, v10, v13
  4. 4

    Clean the signal

    Strip out patterns shared by everything, and background noise seen in the controls.

    v6, v12, v13
  5. 5

    Set the volume

    Choose how strongly to apply the predicted change (I called it lambda, or amplitude).

    v7, v10–v13
  6. 6

    Mark the changed genes

    Make sure enough genes are clearly "called" as changed, in the right direction.

    v7, v10, v12
  7. 7

    Make individual cells

    Turn the average prediction into hundreds of realistic cells with whole-number counts.

    v4, v5

That last step deserves a word. I called the starting count matrix the carrier and the procedure that modifies it the emitter. Two predictions with the exact same average can score quite differently depending on how the counts are spread across individual cells. I didn’t appreciate that at all at the start.

I grouped the versions into six chapters, because several of them were really the same idea being refined. Each chapter below follows the same pattern: what I was thinking, how I built it, what went well, what went wrong, what I tried that didn’t work, and what I carried into the next round.

A quick honesty note. The record of what I did is pretty good for the later versions and patchy for the earliest ones. Where the archive doesn’t say why I did something, I’ve written my best reconstruction and marked it with “Looking back”.

Chapter 1 · v0 to v3: borrowing answers from other cells

September · the starting point
test 0.023v0 0.129v1 0.134v2 0.144v3 0.148

The idea

The simplest honest approach I could think of: if switching off gene X in some well-studied cell line moves genes Y and Z, maybe it moves them in the new cell type too. So take responses that were already measured somewhere else, average them sensibly, and adjust them to the new cell.

How I built it

My very first submission, on 27 September, was just a test to check that the whole upload worked: an identity-style baseline called identity75. It scored 0.0230, which is about what you’d expect.

The early core used five cell types that have been perturbed a lot: H1 (stem cells), K562, RPE1, HepG2 and Jurkat. A first real baseline using just those five, which I called universal_5 and think of as v0, scored 0.1290 the next day.

Then I tried something I still like. I split each response into two parts: its direction (which genes go up or down) and its size (by how much). Related stem-cell experiments (KOLF2.1J, HipSci and hESC) were allowed to vote on the direction, but with a low weight of 0.25 each, while the size stayed decided by the trusted core. A common generator then turned the averages into simulated cells. That became v1, which scored 0.1338 on 30 September.

v2 went much wider. I added a long list of public datasets (Orion HCT116 and HEK293T, CD4 T cells under different conditions, Jiang by cell line and cytokine, PerturbME, Frangieh, Tian, Papalexi and more KOLF samples) and started experimenting with learned transfer between contexts, shrinkage of uncertain estimates, and different ways of turning averages into cells. It scored 0.1441 on 6 October, a clear step up from v1, and PDS jumped from 0.516 to 0.601.

v3 consolidated all that into what became my published reference: 0.1482.

Where the early numbers come from. The scores for the test, v0, v1 and v2 come from the official result emails the challenge sent me, with the four decimals they show. Those emails don’t name the model or the entry, so I matched each one to its version myself, using the dates and my local logs.

What went well

  • I had a full pipeline running end to end and a real number on the board.
  • The direction/size split let me use related data without letting noisy new sources decide how big the effect should be.
  • The local test for the direction-bank idea improved several contexts.

What went wrong

  • The MSE score was 0.0000 in every one of these early submissions. My predicted levels were no closer to reality than the simple reference.
  • I found a bug where a cache of control cells was being reused across different cell contexts. That kind of mistake silently poisons everything downstream.
  • Some reference panels had an undefined MSE score, which made comparisons slippery.
  • My bookkeeping was a mess. For a while I couldn’t even say what v2 had scored: the number was sitting in an email, and the emails don’t say which model they’re about. I also have two different entry IDs recorded for v2.

Tried, didn’t work

  • Transferring KOLF, Jurkat, HipSci and hESC data to HepG2. Each one came out slightly below the baseline: a source that helps one cell type can hurt another.
  • The direction-bank recipe cost a little in HepG2 even while helping elsewhere.

What I took into the next round. Looking back, I think my averages were roughly in the right neighbourhood, but the scores for “which genes changed” were weak. So my next suspicion was the last station of the assembly line: maybe the problem wasn’t what I predicted on average, but how I spread it across individual cells.

Chapter 2 · v4 and v5: same totals, different cells

Reshaping the simulated cells
v4 0.147v5 0.151

The idea

Imagine 400 jars, one per simulated cell, and you have to share out 4,000 sugar cubes for a given gene. You can put ten in each jar, or you can be more realistic and let some jars get more and some fewer, the way real cells naturally vary. The total stays the same, but the pattern is very different. Tests that decide whether a gene “changed” look at that cell-to-cell spread, not only at the average.

How I built it

v4 used a transform called joint_control_copula050_r1. A copula is a statistical tool that copies how values vary together. Here it borrowed the shape of natural variation from real untreated cells and applied it to my predictions, while keeping each gene’s total exactly the same.

v5 was gentler. It only reshaped weak effects (small changes, below 0.085 on a log scale), and it protected the 54 strongest effects, the target gene itself and genes with very few counts. Afterwards it repaired the whole-number totals so every row and column still added up.

The plot twist: my own test was lying to me

My local evaluation said v4 was a big jump: 0.2005 against 0.1614 for the old recipe. I was pretty excited. The official leaderboard said 0.1465, lower than v3.

When I dug in, I found the reason. My local evaluator only looked at a sample of the predicted cells, min(400, number of real cells). Redistributing counts changed which cells ended up in that sample, and that alone created a fake improvement in PDS. Once I fixed it to use all 400 cells, the gain mostly vanished, and what was left came from the “changed genes” metrics, not from PDS or MSE.

This was probably the most useful thing I learned in the whole project, and it came from a failure: a better local score is not a better score. I kept relearning this until the very end.

What went well

  • Found and fixed a real bug in my evaluator. Every later comparison was more trustworthy because of it.
  • v5 nudged the official score up to 0.1507.
  • The v5 count matrix became the carrier that every later version reused. That stability helped a lot.

What went wrong

  • v4 went backwards officially, after looking great locally.
  • MSE score still zero in both versions.
  • v5’s gains were supported by four development contexts, which isn’t the same as an independent confirmation.

Tried, didn’t work

  • Real genome coordinates (GENCODE). I thought knowing where genes sit on the chromosome might help, since neighbouring genes sometimes react together. The effect was −0.0006, basically zero. Biologically plausible doesn’t mean useful.
  • Tree models and TabICL. Gradient-boosted trees and a tabular foundation model reached 0.1646 and 0.1696 locally, a hair above the old recipe and below the copula candidate. Not worth it.
  • Starting from resampled controls instead of the v5 carrier. It lost the predictive starting point that v5 had built.

What I took into the next round. The cells were now in decent shape, and I’d stopped fiddling with that station. Looking back, it was obvious the bigger lever was the response itself. If my borrowed responses were weak or came from the wrong kind of cell, no amount of reshaping would save them. Time to look for better sources.

Chapter 3 · v6 to v9: the right source for each cell

The steady climb
v6 0.171v7 ≈0.194v8 0.208v9 0.208

The idea

Instead of one universal average, build specialised branches, each tuned to a family of cell types, and send every hidden validation context (they were called A, B and C) down the branch whose controls look most like it.

How I built it

v6 had two branches. The H1 branch averaged effects from the genome-wide K562 screen (GWPS), CD4 T cells, HCT116 and HEK293T. The CD4 branch used a centred version of GWPS plus a ridge-regression prediction from Jurkat. Context A went to the CD4 branch and B and C went to H1, chosen by a frozen similarity weighting between their control cells. I also started centring: subtracting part of the response that every target shares, so the predictions don’t all look alike. Result: 0.1705, and for the first time my MSE score was above zero (0.150). That felt good.

v7 worked on the “changed genes” side. I added a floor so that around 3,000 genes per target would be clearly called as changed, with a statistical threshold relaxed from 8 towards 4.5, plus a component for the target gene itself (which obviously should go down). In A, I also recalculated the volume after removing 10 background patterns. Result: about 0.194, and the direction metric (fidelity) turned positive for the first time.

v8 changed only context A. It moved to the H1 multi-source branch and added a genome-wide Jurkat screen (GSE249595). The Jurkat data was blended in gene by gene through a noise gate: the clearer a gene’s effect compared with its noise, the more Jurkat got to say. Result: 0.2084.

v9 added a dataset by Yao et al. (GSE221321) in THP1 cells, a myeloid cell line, stimulated for three hours. It covered 598 targets, but only 12 matched the official list. Result: 0.20844. That’s +0.00006. Basically the same.

What went well

  • The biggest climb of the project: from 0.151 to 0.208 in four versions.
  • MSE score went from zero to 0.221. My predicted levels finally beat the reference.
  • Changing only context A in v8 was a cleaner experiment than usual.

What went wrong

  • In v6 I changed sources, branches and cell generation all at once, so I couldn’t tell which part helped.
  • Routing A to “CD4-like” doesn’t prove A is CD4 cells. It just means the controls looked similar.
  • v9 added a lot of work for almost nothing. Stimulated THP1 cells aren’t resting T cells.
  • I fitted a straight line through two points (v6 and v7) to forecast scores. Two points always fit a line perfectly, which is exactly why it told me nothing.

Tried, didn’t work

  • Adding HipSci and PerturbFate to my best H1 recipe. Local score dropped from 0.3006 to 0.2993 and 0.2987. Related data can dilute a good signal.
  • Glioma (A172, T98G, U87MG) and TeloHAEC variants. They didn’t pass the first promotion checks. I kept them as reference data instead.
  • Jiang 2025. My first audit found only nine targets matching the official list, so I never gave it a real performance test. Low coverage on its own doesn’t prove it would have hurt.

What I took into the next round. Every source I added seemed to help a bit, so the natural thought was: what if I add everything, weighted properly by how reliable it is, and adjust each effect to the baseline of the new cell? You can probably guess where this is going.

Chapter 4 · v10 and v11: the big expansion, and the step back

More is not always better
v10 0.198v11 0.211

The idea

Use far more data, combine it according to how much each source can be trusted, and translate each borrowed effect into the “language” of the new cell’s baseline expression.

How I built it

v10 was my most ambitious version. On top of the four base sources it added Orion and CD4 profiles with no minimum cell count (so rare targets weren’t thrown away), VIPerturb and DLD1 for B and C, Jurkat for A, plus eleven GEO studies and nine L1000 cell-line banks for everything.

To combine them it used inverse-variance weighting for B and C (trust precise sources more) and a random-effects model for A (also allow for real differences between sources). Then it switched on three transformations to fit effects to the new cell: fold-change transfer, a transfer map, and a mask that ignored genes detected in fewer than 5% of the new cell’s controls. It also turned the volume up to 1.10 and raised the changed-gene target from 3,000 to 6,000.

Locally, the GEO plus L1000 setup looked like my best ever, around 0.2314.

Officially: 0.1985. A clear step backwards. The MSE score fell from 0.221 to 0.169.

v11 was the step back. I kept the wider sources but switched off all three baseline transformations and turned the volume back to 0.99. Result: 0.2113, better than both v10 (+0.0128) and v9 (+0.0028). The MSE score hit 0.239, the best it ever got.

What went well

  • v11 recovered and set a new best, with the best “getting the levels right” score of the whole project.
  • Keeping the extra data but removing the risky transformations was a useful, fairly clean contrast.
  • v11 became my reference for expression accuracy from then on.

What went wrong

  • v10 changed many things at once. Baseline scaling, the louder volume and the way I picked local test panels are all plausible culprits, and I never ran a clean one-cause-at-a-time test officially.
  • My forecast for v11 was 0.22685. Reality was 0.2113. Even after v10, my local tests were still too optimistic.
  • v11’s reach and overlap metrics were worse than v9’s.

Tried, didn’t work

  • Mixing all sources in bulk. About 0.2300 locally versus 0.2314 for GEO plus L1000. More wasn’t better.
  • Transferring a “common perturbation response” (the part every knockdown seems to share). It agreed poorly across cell types and made local metrics worse.
  • Removing the minimum cell count everywhere. It recovers rare targets, but it doesn’t fix a weak knockdown, a wrong control group, a double perturbation or an incompatible unit. It just lets those problems in.

There was also a sneaky one. An effect with a declared variance of zero gets infinite trust under inverse-variance weighting, so one bad row can take over a gene. I had to add an uncertainty floor. Data plumbing like this was a big part of the job.

What I took into the next round. v11 got the levels right but wasn’t great at telling targets apart. The question became: can I keep v11’s accuracy and make the targets more distinguishable? My idea was to turn down the background noise.

Chapter 5 · v12 and v13: turning down the background noise

The best version, and the one that tried too hard
v12 0.2166v13 0.212

The idea

Even untreated cells differ from each other in big, recurring ways. Things like how far along the cell cycle a cell is, or how big or stressed it is. Those patterns can show up in a prediction and make every target look a bit alike. If I find the main patterns of variation in the control cells and remove them from my predictions, the target-specific part should stand out. It’s like noise-cancelling headphones: find the hum, subtract it.

How I built it

v12 took up to 3,000 untreated controls per context, put them on a common scale (log1p of counts per 50,000), and found their 15 main patterns of variation with an SVD (a standard way of finding principal components, or PCs). Those 15 patterns were projected out of the predicted effects, then the volume was recalculated, which landed at 0.90, 0.925 and 0.975 for A, B and C. The changed-gene target went up from 6,000 to 8,000.

Result: 0.2166, my best official score. The six metrics show exactly what changed against v11: PDS jumped from 0.655 to 0.743, while the MSE score slipped from 0.239 to 0.211. Removing background patterns made the targets much easier to tell apart, at a small cost in getting the levels right, and the trade was worth it overall.

v13 tried to push further. It weighted each gene by how much the sources agreed with each other, removed 8 patterns from the receiver plus an extra 15-pattern subspace from the VIPerturb controls, turned the volume up to 1.05 and allowed it to go much higher for some genes. Result: 0.2124, below v12.

The individual metrics tell the story. v13 pushed PDS a little higher still, to 0.757 (v12 had 0.743), but its MSE score fell further, to 0.192 (v12 had 0.211), and NMAE and reach dropped too. A small gain in telling targets apart, paid for with a bigger loss in getting the levels right. The overall score cares about the balance, and v13 lost it.

I also checked the trade-off locally on a held-out panel (VIPerturb B/C, with separate control groups for generating and scoring). These are raw local numbers, not official scores:

Variant PDS ↑ MSE ↓ Reach ↑
v11 0.578 1.114 0.510
50/50 mix 0.580 1.026 0.538
15 patterns removed, 6,000 genes 0.602 1.123 0.504
15 patterns removed, 8,000 genes 0.602 1.166 0.530

Removing patterns helped tell targets apart. A mixture got the levels closest. No single variant won everything, and this panel couldn’t settle what the hidden contexts would do.

What went well

  • v12 was my best official result (0.2166), and its package and generator are fully preserved.
  • The background-removal idea did what I hoped for PDS: 0.655 to 0.743 in one step.
  • v13 taught me, very clearly, what over-amplifying costs.

What went wrong

  • Removing control patterns also removed some real signal: the MSE score fell from 0.239 to 0.211.
  • v13 changed reliability weights, removed patterns and turned up the volume. Too much volume is my main suspect, but I can’t prove it was the only cause.
  • Two different recipes were both called “v13” at some point. The one I submitted was candidate c12133. Naming things properly is underrated.

Tried, didn’t work

  • More patterns removed, or more weight on VIPerturb. Small, inconsistent changes or regressions. Turning a dial further isn’t progress by itself.
  • Reliability scores that included the receiver’s own data. They looked amazing because they were contaminated. Rejected.
  • Direct effects without the carrier. Looked promising against a control-only baseline, but worse on realistic baselines. Changing the starting point can fake a gain.

What I took into the next round. The newest version wasn’t the best. For the final round, I decided ahead of time that if I couldn’t show something beat v12, I’d go with v12.

Chapter 6 · v14-DEF: trust the evidence, not the newest idea

The final test delivery · 24 October
v14-DEF published · score not visible

The idea

The final round used three new hidden contexts, D, E and F. Again, only their untreated controls were available. I wanted to use the strongest recipe I had evidence for, rather than whatever I’d built most recently.

How I built it

I rebuilt the controls and target lists for the final panel. Looking at the controls gave some clues: D resembled HL60 and other myeloid cells, E was close to the glioma line U87MG, and F looked mesenchymal or fibroblast-like, near AC16 heart cells. These were clues, not identities. Looking like a cell line doesn’t give you its perturbation responses.

D used the recipe from context A, and E and F used the recipe from B and C. I ran a quick comparison on look-alike datasets (Yao THP1, U87MG and T98G, with 18, 21 and 21 usable targets). It couldn’t produce a fair winner, because some MSE values were undefined.

So the rule I’d set in advance kicked in: fall back to the best official historical evidence, which was v12. The final delivery used v12’s recipe (15 patterns removed, volume ratio 0.99, 8,000 changed genes) with the new controls and targets, under the name pedrito-amor-v14-DEF.

The upload was about 3.2 GB, checksum verified, finished on 24 October at 14:21 UTC. The server shows it as published, with no score visible yet.

What went well

  • I decided the fallback rule before seeing results, which stopped me from fooling myself at the last minute.
  • It’s a genuine new prediction with new controls and targets, not a relabelled old file.
  • The upload was verified byte for byte.

What I still don’t know

  • Whether it worked. There’s no visible score, and “published” isn’t the same as zero or as good.
  • Whether v12’s recipe transfers to D, E and F. There were no real perturbed answers for those cells to check against.

Behind the scenes: 14,218 tries

The versions are only the tip of it. Behind them I logged 14,218 results in eleven log journals. To be clear, that’s 14,218 rows in log files, not 14,218 independent models or validated experiments. Some are tiny variations of each other.

The biggest one was a fast search over 10,000 continuous combinations: mixtures of versions, patterns removed, volume and masks. 2,857 of them passed an eligibility check, which is not the same as 2,857 improvements. The winner was 90% v10 plus 10% v11, with some extra pattern removal, a mask on low-expression genes and the volume at 0.85. On 60 internal test cases it improved PDS from 0.592 to 0.605. But it was never turned into actual cells with whole-number counts, so its real MSE remained unknown. I didn’t submit it.

A separate source-combining recipe did go all the way to real counts on a training panel, and it beat both v11 and the 15-pattern recipe there, on PDS (0.611 against 0.566 and 0.598) and on raw error (1.081 against 1.165 and 1.211). It’s the one local result I’d still like to test properly. But it was picked on training data, and its normalised MSE score was still zero.

Did the big AI models help?

Of course I had to try the fancy stuff. In total I tested twelve alternative routes, including some of the best-known models for single-cell perturbation:

CPA

Separates "which cell" from "what was done to it", then recombines them. No error improvement over v11.

scGen

Treats a perturbation as a shift inside a learned summary space of cells. No improvement.

GEARS

Uses a map of how genes relate functionally. Some fits finished, but the long queue was stopped for cost, so it never got a complete row.

STATE

Learns responses from whole sets of observed cells. Trained on a smaller subset; worse error than v11. A bigger all-cells run was prepared but never finished.

PRESAGE

Predicts responses using structured gene knowledge. Run with a simplified knowledge setup. No gain.

scLAMBDA

Another learned-latent-space model for perturbations. No gain.

Ridge

The deliberately boring baseline, to check whether complexity buys anything. No gain either.

STRING & GenePT readers

Small networks that guess a gene's response from similar genes, using protein interaction maps or text-based gene descriptions.

Geneformer & scGPT

Large pretrained models of gene expression, used frozen, with a small trained layer on top. Not fine-tuned for perturbations.

PIE

Arc Institute's perturbation model, and the one I took furthest: I trained it from scratch on public data plus my own banks, and also ran Arc's published checkpoint. Neither beat v11. Full story below.

Here’s how their raw error compared with my three submitted recipes on two historical test panels. Lower is better; the dashed line marks v11.

None of the twelve routes beat v11 on error in these panels. I want to be careful about what that means, though. It’s a finding about my adapters, my training scope and these panels, not proof that these methods are bad. Several of them only produced predictions for a small share of the target-by-gene table (about 16.5% for CPA and 12.5% for STATE on H1). Four of them trained on seven selected groups, about 71,000 cells out of nearly 339,000 available. And using a frozen foundation model with a small layer on top isn’t the same as fine-tuning it properly. Given more time and compute, I’d love to see this comparison done fairly.

PIE: the one I took all the way

PIE got more of my time than any other model, so it deserves its own story. It isn’t in the chart above because it was tested on a different panel.

First attempts (10 to 18 October). I downloaded PIE’s preprocessed public datasets and started with a high-memory Colab pilot on CD4 T cells, then a version trained only on my own data banks. These early runs hit problems with scale and with how changed genes were called, so I didn’t trust their numbers.

Training PIE from scratch on everything (18 to 19 October). Then I went big. I kept PIE’s architecture (a Perceiver network with 512 latent slots and 14 layers in total) but started every response parameter from zero. I didn’t load any checkpoint that had already seen real responses. I trained it on five public datasets together: Replogle/Nadig, Tahoe-100M, Jiang, ARC VCC 2025 and X-Atlas/Orion. That’s about 76,000 public profiles, plus 45,000 profiles from my own banks, around 68 GB of inputs and 274,000 training rows in all. It also used PIE’s eleven knowledge sources, things like protein language-model embeddings (ESM2), STRING, DepMap, gene and perturbation text descriptions, L1000, PRISM and JUMP. Each kind of measurement kept its own output head, so I never had to mix units.

0.864best validation error ratio (1.0 = predicting no change)

The validation error fell fast, from 5.22 at step 50 to about 0.87 by step 500, and then it plateaued between 0.86 and 0.92 for the next 5,750 steps. So I stopped it by hand and froze the best checkpoint. It had seen every training task once, but not every gene of every profile, and it never finished its planned training budget.

Plugging it into my pipeline (19 October). I built a bridge that turned PIE’s predictions into count cells and mixed them with v11 at four strengths (0%, 25%, 50% and 100% PIE). Then I scored them on held-out VIPerturb targets: 21 to choose a mix, plus 27 sealed ones kept for confirmation. The verdict was “no comparable six-metric score, keep v11”. One of the local reference points broke: the Jaccard anchor was higher for a generic answer than for real replicates. So the comparison couldn’t produce a fair overall number at all.

Running Arc’s own published checkpoint (19 to 20 October). In case my training was the problem, I also ran the checkpoint Arc published for the Replogle K562 benchmark. First I made sure I was running it correctly, and it reproduced their published numbers almost exactly:

The biggest difference was 0.0007, inside the 0.005 tolerance I’d set beforehand. So the model ran exactly as published. The trouble came when I moved it to my problem. On 170 VIPerturb K562 targets, PIE’s predicted changes were barely closer to the truth than the same predictions shuffled between targets (median error ratio 4.26 against 4.33). A plain generic response did better (2.32), and the cosine similarity between PIE’s changes and the real ones was essentially zero. About 37% of what it predicted was a common change shared by every target. In other words, PIE knew a lot about the benchmark it was trained for, and very little that was target-specific about this new study.

I still compared it fairly against v11, with a stricter rule I wrote down before running that comparison: a paired bootstrap across targets, a correction for testing several candidates at once, and margins for every metric. The candidates were v11, pure PIE, two PIE/v11 mixes (10% and 25% PIE) and a “null” mix with no PIE signal at all. The result was “keep v11”, because the metrics didn’t have enough targets in common for a fair comparison (for example, only 69% for fidelity, below the 80% I required).

So yes, I tested PIE properly: trained from scratch, run as published, bridged into my pipeline and compared under a rule fixed in advance. It just never earned a place over v11, and I never submitted a PIE-based version. Two honest caveats: my own training run stopped before its full budget, and I couldn’t verify how much PIE’s pretraining data overlapped with the data I evaluated on.

The practice exam problem

If I had to name the single theme of this project, it’s this one. My local tests were like a practice exam. They were useful, but they didn’t always predict the real exam.

A local evaluator can do the arithmetic perfectly and still rank recipes differently from the hidden competition. Which targets you test on, which sources you leave out, how you split the controls and which starting carrier you use all change the answer. Some examples I ran into:

  • The official order was v11 > v9 > v10. On my 300-target VIPerturb panel, v10 was also the worst, but v11 didn’t beat v9. My 24-target CD4 panel didn’t reproduce the official order either. I didn’t force them to agree.
  • Breaking the assembly line into stages showed that the final cell-spreading step alone could make the error worse: for v10 on VIPerturb, raw MSE went from 1.083 to 1.199 at that step, with identical gene totals.
  • A supposed factor-of-100 error in the CD4 data turned out not to exist. What actually mattered was how things were normalised: per cell or after summing.
  • Fourteen official targets on one platform always had an undefined MSE score, so I kept them separate. Adding 544 other targets didn’t make them valid.

Working with AI assistants

I used Codex and Claude a lot: to find datasets, write extractors and adapters, propose experiments, coordinate audits and job queues, and set up Colab notebooks. I also wrote self-contained prompts for ChatGPT Pro. They were genuinely helpful, but I treated their suggestions as ideas to test, not as evidence. I set the priorities and decided what got submitted.

Honestly, a big chunk of the time went into engineering rather than science: package versions that refused to cooperate, cache locks on Google Drive, losing everything after a Colab reconnect, files being hashed again and again, slow graph preparation for GEARS, fits that never finished. The fixes were unglamorous: isolated environments, checkpoints, cache keys based on file content, logs per phase, timers, and only reusing results that were marked complete. A smoke test passing never counted as a scientific win.

Glossary

Control (NTC)
Cells that went through the experiment without any gene being switched off. They show the normal starting point.
Source and receiver
A source is an experiment someone already measured. The receiver is the new cell type I'm predicting for.
Perturbation
Switching off (here, quieting with CRISPRi) one gene and seeing what happens.
Proxy
My local stand-in for the hidden official evaluation. Only as good as how well its conclusions transfer.
Control PCs
The main patterns of natural variation among untreated cells. Removing them can reduce noise, or remove real signal.
Lambda / amplitude
The volume knob: how strongly the predicted change is applied.
CP50K
A common scale where each cell's counts are rescaled to add up to 50,000, then log-transformed. Mixing scales silently is a classic bug.
Leakage
Accidentally using the answers (or something derived from them) to build the prediction. Makes results look great and mean nothing.
Carrier and emitter
The starting count matrix, and the procedure that modifies it into the final simulated cells.
Pseudobulk
The average of many cells treated as one sample. Handy for comparing, but it hides cell-to-cell variation.
